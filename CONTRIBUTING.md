Thank you for wanting to join in! Before you step forth, read the following in full. You'll need to know these before you start (all of this also extends to your own projects you share with us in our community):

You're not required to type out the code letter by letter by hand, just as no one requires you to punch holes on a physical paper card nowadays; Correct code is correct code. But you must be able to explain why you made the choices you did in why the code should be written that way and defend why it should be like that (yourself).

You are encouraged to ask questions, even (especially) stupid ones, and to ask them to anyone (including AI) about anything, though as with all questions asked, you must evaluate the answer to see if it is correct. Questions are good when learning, no matter how dumb they might sound to someone who already knows, it's only something that is too bad that people do not have equal access to this based on where they happen to live.

You must write all non-code text yourself, including documentation, commit messages, and communication. I use multiple languages myself, and I want to reiterate that if you post in your native language you will not be disadvantaged in any way during communication, nor if you have grammar errors if you post in English; and this goes for readers, please privately treat such messages with equal weight, noting when there were parts you didn't get or if you needed, for when it is in a different language, to use AI to translate in order to understand. Be understanding of someone else’s understanding if they had to pass it through translation first in either direction. AI has been pushed for the last few years to have stronger logical reasoning, but not human prose at all, and human nuance is routinely washed away and missed, which is why we have this rule.

The spirit of this project is one of trying, failing, and trying again with more mirth. If you believe that true programming is writing perfect code on the first try in one-shot without swallowing mistakes, no matter who was the one who pointed it out either, you will not do good here, and I highly suggest finding some place else. We are civil when we do so and expect it from everyone. This is important for our culture. We are proud of our code, but we are proud of the evolution of the code and how it kept improving as we worked on it, not that we necessarily found it to be a cakewalk, since the less solved a problem is, the less there will be a correct answer already written to begin with.

If you are participating in the project, we expect that you are here for the project. Those who are not contributing to the project should realize that someone who is not working on the project is going to have a worse idea of what is involved in working on the project, compared to someone who is working on the project. It appears self-evident, but often gets lost in conversation when it begins to drift.

You are expected to respect others' situations. Do not presume that your circumstance is universal, and do not judge others with that reasoning. It is not defensible, as you will be met with a reminder that said assumptions are far from universal, and if you repeatedly do this, it will be counted as problematic conduct towards others.

Pro- anti- framing is unacceptable here. Do not label people, including yourself. You *will* be kicked for it. You should neither accuse someone of being such a binary label, nor advocate for such a binary label. Discussions should consider mechanisms. Labels are tribalism, and there are more fun tribes to have fun socially with than using nebulous, accusatory, region and country-specific internet catch-phrases that reduce people to labels. It is giving yourself permission to treat people as not human, which we do not tolerate. We treat people as people, and always discuss mechanisms and details. Do not forget this.

Code, or more specifically language (language in writing, abstract language, etc), is already covered above separately due to its nature as something much closer to pedagogy. If the rules above are related to pedagogy, the rules from here are related to tools and processes. Text-prompted asset generation in its current form has too many blanks filled in for you, where the human's intentions are rarely complete and the machine fills in 99%. This is not allowed here, but the nuanced rule is for exceptions like Vocaloid 6, where human intention *is* complete going in and simply transformed, so that we don't accidentally exclude Vocaloid 6 and others like it which pass the rule easily. The three-point rule we use is: 1) Is the user's intention complete? => yes, 2) Is the user's intention preserved and transformed? => yes, 3) Is it technologically using the same matrix multiplication and reverse propagation underneath for implementation? => yes. If a technology is a yes on points 1 and 2, they are allowed. Vocaloid is additionally yes on point 3, but this does not disqualify it for allowance. This three-point rule will not change as new technologies appear, please evaluate them using the rule, and that is the answer to whether they are allowed in this community of ours.
